**Quick answer:** compound interest is interest earned on both your original money and on the interest it has already earned. ₹1,00,000 at 8% a year, compounded monthly, becomes about **₹1,48,985 in 5 years**, **₹2,21,964 in 10 years** and **₹4,92,680 in 20 years**. You can test your own numbers in our free [Compound Interest Calculator](/compound-interest-calculator/).

## Simple interest versus compound interest

With **simple interest**, you earn interest only on the original amount. ₹1,00,000 at 8% simple interest earns ₹8,000 every year, whether it is year one or year twenty. After 10 years you have ₹1,80,000.

With **compound interest**, each year's interest is added to the balance, and the next year's interest is calculated on the larger amount. After 10 years at 8% compounded yearly you have ₹2,15,892, which is about ₹36,000 more than simple interest produces. The longer the time, the wider the gap becomes.

## The compound interest formula

**A = P × (1 + r ÷ n)^(n × t)**

- **A** is the final amount.
- **P** is the principal, the starting amount.
- **r** is the annual interest rate written as a decimal (8% = 0.08).
- **n** is how many times a year interest is added.
- **t** is the time in years.

The interest you earned is A − P.

### Worked example

Let P = ₹1,00,000, r = 8%, compounded yearly (n = 1) for 5 years:

A = 1,00,000 × (1.08)⁵ = 1,00,000 × 1.46933 = **₹1,46,933**.

Interest earned is ₹46,933. With simple interest it would be ₹40,000, so compounding added almost ₹7,000.

## Why time is the strongest ingredient

Compounding is slow at first and then accelerates. Here is ₹1,00,000 at 8% compounded in different ways:

| Years | Yearly | Quarterly | Monthly |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | ₹1,46,933 | ₹1,48,595 | ₹1,48,985 |
| 10 | ₹2,15,892 | ₹2,20,804 | ₹2,21,964 |
| 20 | ₹4,66,096 | ₹4,87,544 | ₹4,92,680 |

In the first 10 years the money roughly doubles. In the next 10 years it more than doubles again. The balance grows by about ₹1.15 lakh in the first decade but about ₹2.5 lakh in the second, even though you added nothing.

## Compounding frequency: a small but real effect

Interest can be added yearly, half-yearly, quarterly, monthly or even daily. More frequent compounding increases the final amount slightly because interest starts earning interest sooner. The difference between yearly and monthly is about 2.8% of the final value over 10 years in our example, and it is much smaller than the effect of the rate or the time horizon. Fixed deposits in India typically compound quarterly, while many savings accounts credit interest on a different schedule, so always check the terms.

## The Rule of 72: a mental shortcut

To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the annual interest rate.

- At 6%, money doubles in about 12 years.
- At 8%, in about 9 years (the exact answer is 9.01).
- At 12%, in about 6 years.

The rule also works backwards. If you want to double your money in 6 years, you need roughly 12% a year. It is an approximation that works best for rates between about 4% and 15%.

- **Lump sum investments.** The [Lumpsum Calculator](/lumpsum-calculator/) uses yearly compounding, a common way to project a one-time investment at an assumed annual return.
- **Savings accounts and PPF.** Interest accumulates and earns further interest.
- **Debt.** Compounding works against you on credit card balances and some loans. Unpaid interest added to the balance can make a debt grow quickly, which is why paying down high-rate debt first is powerful.

## Common mistakes

1. **Mixing up the rate and period.** A monthly rate and an annual rate are not interchangeable. If you use a monthly rate, n × t must count months.
2. **Ignoring tax and inflation.** Interest may be taxed, and rising prices reduce what the money buys. At 8% interest and 6% inflation your real growth is only about 2%.
3. **Assuming returns are guaranteed.** Bank deposits have fixed, contractual rates, while investments such as mutual funds do not. A calculator with a 12% input projects a scenario and does not promise an outcome.
4. **Starting late.** Delaying by five years can cost far more than saving extra later. In the table above, the last ten years of a 20-year deposit produce more growth than the first ten.

## Key takeaways

- Compound interest earns interest on interest, so growth accelerates with time.
- Time and rate matter far more than compounding frequency.
- The Rule of 72 gives a quick estimate of doubling time.
- Compounding rewards savers and punishes borrowers, so understand which side you are on.
