## Why forms ask for tiny images

Job portals, exam registrations, government forms and e-commerce uploads often set strict file-size limits, commonly 20KB for a signature, 50KB for a passport photo and 100KB to 200KB for documents. Small files save the site's storage and bandwidth and make uploads work on slow connections. The catch is that a modern phone camera produces photos of several megabytes, so you need to shrink them considerably.

## What decides an image's file size

Three things control the size of a JPEG:

1. **Dimensions (pixels).** A 4000 × 3000 photo has 12 million pixels. A 400 × 300 version has 120,000, one hundred times fewer. Reducing dimensions is the biggest single saving.
2. **Compression quality.** JPEG lets you trade detail for size. A quality setting of 90% keeps near-original detail, while 40% gives a much smaller file with visible softness.
3. **Content.** A plain background and smooth skin compress well. Sharp text, fine patterns and noisy low-light photos compress poorly because there is more detail to store.

Because of the first point, the best order is to **resize first, then compress**.

## How our compressor reaches your target

When you choose a file, the tool first saves it as a JPEG at high quality and checks the size. If it is over your target, it lowers the quality in small steps, down to roughly 30%. If the file is still too big, it reduces the image dimensions by about 15% and tries again, repeating until it fits or the image becomes very small. It stops at the first result under your limit, so you get the best quality that fits instead of an over-compressed file.

Everything runs locally in your browser using your device's own image processing, so the photo never leaves your computer or phone.

## Tips to keep quality high at small sizes

- **Crop and resize to the required dimensions first.** If a form wants a 200 × 230 pixel photo, resize to that before compressing. Our [Resize Image](/resize-image/) tool keeps the proportions, and the [Passport Photo Maker](/passport-photo-maker/) helps with standard photo sizes.
- **Start from a sharp original.** Blurry or noisy photos compress badly and look worse afterwards.
- **Use good lighting and a plain background** for portraits. Flat backgrounds compress to far smaller files.
- **Avoid compressing twice.** Each pass through a lossy format loses detail. Compress once from the original, not from an already compressed copy.
- **Keep the original.** Save your full-size photo and work on a copy.

## Signatures and scanned documents

A signature on white paper is easy: it is mostly white space, so even 20KB is realistic at a small size. Photograph or scan it in good light, crop tightly and run it through the 20KB tool. For documents, text becomes blurry if the image is shrunk too far, so for 100KB targets start with a scan no larger than needed. For multi-page documents consider combining pages into a [PDF](/image-to-pdf/) instead.

## JPEG, PNG and WebP: which format?

- **JPEG** suits photographs and is the format our compressor produces. It cannot store transparency.
- **PNG** suits screenshots, logos and graphics with sharp edges or transparency, but photos in PNG are large.
- **WebP** is a modern format with smaller files than JPEG at similar quality, but some old forms and portals do not accept it.

If a site asks for JPG and you have a PNG or WebP, use our [Image Converter](/image-converter/) or the [PNG to JPG converter](/png-to-jpg/) first. Note that when a transparent PNG becomes a JPEG, the transparent areas turn white.

## Troubleshooting

**The file is still too large.** The image may be very big or full of detail. Resize it to the required pixel dimensions first, then compress again from the original.

**The result looks blocky.** The target is probably too small for that picture. Reduce the dimensions deliberately so there are fewer pixels to compress, or check whether the form really needs the smallest option.

**The portal rejects the file.** Check the required format (often JPG or JPEG, not JPE or PNG), the maximum and sometimes minimum size, and the pixel dimensions.

**The colours look different.** JPEG conversion can shift colours slightly. This is rarely visible in photos, but if exact colours matter, use a larger size limit.
