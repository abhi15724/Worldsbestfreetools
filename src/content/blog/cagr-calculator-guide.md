**Quick answer:** CAGR (compound annual growth rate) is the steady yearly growth rate that would take an investment from its starting value to its ending value over a given number of years. If ₹1,00,000 becomes ₹2,50,000 in 5 years, the CAGR is **20.11%** a year. Try your own figures in our free [CAGR Calculator](/cagr-calculator/).

## What CAGR means

Real investments rarely grow in a straight line. One year might bring a 30% gain, the next a 10% loss. CAGR smooths that bumpy path into one equivalent annual rate, answering a simple question: "If the investment had grown at the same rate every year, what rate would produce the same end result?" It is not the rate the investment actually earned each year, which varied, but a single number that describes the journey from start to finish.

This makes CAGR the standard way to compare growth across different periods, such as a stock's price over 3 years against a fund's value over 7 years, or a company's revenue over a decade.

## The CAGR formula

**CAGR = (Final value ÷ Initial value)^(1 ÷ n) − 1**

Here n is the number of years. Multiply by 100 to express it as a percentage.

### Example 1: an investment that grows 2.5 times

You invest ₹1,00,000 and after 5 years it is worth ₹2,50,000.

1. Final ÷ Initial = 2,50,000 ÷ 1,00,000 = 2.5
2. Raise to the power 1 ÷ 5 = 0.2: 2.5^0.2 = 1.2011
3. Subtract 1: 0.2011, which is **20.11% a year**

### Example 2: how fast does money double?

If an investment doubles in 7 years, CAGR = 2^(1/7) − 1 = **10.41%** a year. This agrees with the Rule of 72, which says 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10.3%.

### Example 3: a negative CAGR

An investment falls from ₹1,00,000 to ₹80,000 over 4 years. CAGR = (0.8)^(0.25) − 1 = −5.4% a year. A negative result simply shows an average yearly decline.

## CAGR versus absolute return

**Absolute return** is the total gain over the whole period: (2,50,000 − 1,00,000) ÷ 1,00,000 = **150%**. It tells you how much you made but not how long it took. A 150% gain in 5 years is excellent, while 150% over 30 years is quite ordinary. CAGR fixes this by converting the gain to a yearly rate, so you can compare:

| Investment | Period | Absolute return | CAGR |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 years | 150% | 20.11% |
| B | 10 years | 200% | 11.61% |
| C | 7 years | 100% | 10.41% |

Investment B has the highest absolute return of the three (200%), yet its yearly growth of 11.61% is far below A's 20.11%, because it took twice as long. Only CAGR makes that visible. For B, the figures are ₹1,00,000 growing to ₹3,00,000 in 10 years.

## Where CAGR is used

- **Mutual funds and stocks:** reporting a fund's growth over 3, 5 or 10 years.
- **Business metrics:** revenue, profit or user growth across several years.
- **Savings goals:** working out the return you need to reach a target. For example, to turn ₹5 lakh into ₹10 lakh in 6 years you need about 12.2% a year.
- **Comparing options:** lining up two investments or two companies over the same period.

## Limitations: when CAGR misleads

1. **It hides volatility.** Two investments can have the same CAGR yet very different ups and downs along the way. A smooth 10% a year and a rollercoaster ending at the same value look identical in CAGR.
2. **It depends on the start and end dates.** Pick a starting point just after a crash and CAGR looks great; pick one just before and it looks poor. Always check the period and compare over several windows.
3. **It assumes a single investment.** CAGR works for a lump sum, with one starting amount and one ending amount. If you invest regularly, as in a SIP, or add or withdraw money along the way, CAGR is inaccurate. Use **XIRR**, which uses the date of each cash flow. For SIP planning, our [SIP Calculator](/sip-calculator/) estimates future value from a monthly amount and expected return.
4. **It is backward-looking.** A past CAGR does not predict future returns.
5. **It ignores inflation, costs and tax.** A 7% CAGR with 6% inflation is only about a 1% real gain.

## How to use the CAGR calculator

1. Enter the **initial value**, the amount at the start.
2. Enter the **final value**, the amount at the end.
3. Enter the number of **years**. You can use decimals, so 2.5 years is fine.
4. Read the CAGR, then compare it to a benchmark such as inflation or a fixed deposit rate.

You can also work backwards: if you know the CAGR you want and the time you have, the formula Final = Initial × (1 + CAGR)ⁿ tells you what an investment must grow to. The [Compound Interest Calculator](/compound-interest-calculator/) does this for you.

## Key takeaways

- CAGR converts total growth into a smooth yearly rate so different periods can be compared.
- Formula: (Final ÷ Initial)^(1/years) − 1.
- It works for a single investment, not for SIPs or irregular cash flows; use XIRR for those.
- It hides volatility and depends heavily on the chosen dates.
